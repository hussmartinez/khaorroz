# Mlukhiyeh ma' Ruz / ملوخية مع رز

> ⬅ [README](README.md) · [Syria](provincial_syria.md) · [Lebanon](provincial_lebanon.md) · [Palestine](provincial_palestine.md)

> ⚠️ **Source-confidence note:** the Syrian whole-leaf style (dried leaves, served over vermicelli rice) is documented in Arabic recipe media; a specifically **Damascene** source was not found, so city-level attributions below are flagged. The Egyptian minced-leaf soup is a separate tradition, noted only for contrast.

## 1. Geographic & Texture Profile
*   **Primary Geographic Zone:** Syria (Damascus and the west), Lebanon and Palestine; Ramadan iftar tables.
*   **Dish Type & Texture:** Rice with stew (*ruz w yakhneh*). **Whole** jute leaves (*mlukhiyeh*) stewed in chicken (or lamb) broth with garlic and coriander, ladled over rice; glossy and slightly viscous, sharpened with lemon and raw onion in vinegar.
*   **Traditional Cooking Vessel & Fuel:** A large pot for the broth and a frying pan for the garlic–coriander *ta'leyeh*; stovetop.

## 2. Historical & Cultural Context
*Mlukhiyeh* (jute mallow, *Corchorus olitorius*) is eaten across the Arab world, but its form splits by region. **Egypt** chops the leaves finely into a smooth green soup. **Syria and Lebanon** cook the leaves **whole**, often from the **dried** leaves stored after the summer harvest, in a broth with chicken or lamb. The stew is then spooned over rice (often vermicelli rice), with lemon and onions in vinegar at the table. In Syria it is a classic **Ramadan iftar** dish, described in Lebanese and Arabic food media as "a royal dish" for breaking the fast.

The dish's backbone is the *ta'leyeh*: crushed garlic and coriander (fresh and dried) fried in ghee until fragrant, then stirred through the leaves. Roasted garlic and onions blended into the broth deepen it further in some modern Syrian recipes.

**Culinary science:** jute leaves contain mucilaginous polysaccharides that thicken the broth. Cooking them whole, briefly blanching dried leaves, and adding lemon (acid) limits the "slime" and keeps a silky, not stringy, texture. Frying the garlic and coriander in fat carries their fat-soluble aromatics into every spoonful.

## 3. Local Town-by-Town Variations (Crucial)
*   **Syrian whole-leaf (dried), the documented standard:** Soaked or boiled dried leaves, chicken broth, garlic and fresh plus dried coriander fried in samneh, lemon; served with vermicelli rice.
*   **Damascus ⚠️:** Lamb as well as chicken is common in household accounts; generous lemon and onions in vinegar on the side.
*   **Lebanon:** Fresh leaves in summer; chicken and lamb; toasted bread and rice alongside.
*   **Palestine:** Often whole leaves with chicken; in Gaza, chopped leaves closer to the Egyptian style ⚠️.
*   **Egypt (contrast):** Minced leaves, a soup rather than a stew, with *ta'leya* of garlic and coriander; also served with rice.
*   **Lebanese style (*mloukhieh*):** The leaves are kept **whole**, not minced as in Egypt, giving a dish of leaves in broth. Its flavour rests on fresh and dried **coriander fried with crushed garlic** and plenty of lemon (added "so the texture does not change"), with chicken boiled with cinnamon, cardamom and bay. Served over vermicelli rice, often with toasted bread and onion in vinegar.
*   **Lebanese vs Egyptian vs Shami (Asharq Al-Awsat):** Lebanese = coriander and garlic; Egyptians add tomato; Shami (Damascus) cooks use bay and cardamom in the broth. A Georgetown essay contrasts Egyptian abundance (bread, vinegar, onions, soupiness) with Lebanese restraint, though many Lebanese homes also serve bread and vinegar-onion.

### Palestinian style (mlukhiyyeh w ruz)
*(Merged from the Palestine research.)*

*   **Galilee (Saffuriya, Saʿsaʿ tradition):** Celebrated for the dish. Families now largely in refugee communities in Lebanon and Syria ⚠️ (style details not documented).
*   **West Bank:** Whole leaves, chicken or lamb, plenty of garlic and coriander, lemon; served with vermicelli rice.
*   **Gaza (⚠️):** Finely chopped (Egyptian-style), with green chilli and lemon; also eaten with bread.
*   **Ramadan:** Green mulukhiyah is a classic iftar dish on Palestinian tables.

## 4. The Master Recipe (4-Person Batch Blueprint)
*Variant represented: **Syrian dried whole-leaf mlukhiyeh with chicken**, after published Syrian recipes.*

### The Flavor Base (El Fondo)
| Ingredient | Quantity |
|---|---|
| Dried mlukhiyeh leaves | 250 g |
| Chicken, jointed | 1 (≈ 1.3 kg) |
| Onion, carrot, cinnamon stick (broth) | 1 / 1 / 2 |
| Samneh (ghee) | 3 tbsp |
| Garlic, crushed | 1 head |
| Fresh coriander, chopped | 1 cup |
| Dried ground coriander | 1 tsp |
| Lemon juice | 60 ml |
| Salt | to taste |

### Assembly Ratios
| Element | Quantity |
|---|---|
| Vermicelli rice to serve: rice + vermicelli | 300 g + 50 g |
| Chicken broth for the stew | ≈ 1.5 L |
| Onions in vinegar (side) | 1 onion, chopped, in 100 ml vinegar |

### Step-by-Step Cooking Blueprint
1. **Leaves:** Soak the dried leaves in warm water for 1–2 hours, changing the water, or boil them for 20 minutes; drain and rinse well.
2. **Broth:** Simmer the chicken with the onion, carrot and cinnamon for 45 minutes; skim; keep the broth and the chicken.
3. **Ta'leyeh:** Fry the garlic and half the fresh coriander in the samneh until fragrant; add the dried coriander.
4. **Stew:** Add the leaves to the pan and fry them for a few minutes, then add the chicken pieces, the broth and the lemon juice; simmer 30–40 minutes. Add the remaining coriander at the end.
5. **Rice:** Toast the vermicelli in butter until golden, add the rice, then boiling salted water (1.5 : 1); cover and cook 15 minutes.
6. **Serve:** Rice in the bowl, stew ladled over, lemon and onions in vinegar alongside.
