# A History of Rice in Thailand / ประวัติข้าวไทย

> ⬅ [README](README.md)

In Thai, "to eat" is *kin khao*, literally "to eat rice", and a meal's other dishes are simply *kap khao*, "things to go with rice". This is a sourced narrative of how rice shaped the Thai world: prehistoric villages, Mon and Khmer kingdoms, Sukhothai and Lanna, the trading capital of Ayutthaya, the royal kitchens of Bangkok, the 19th-century export boom and the jasmine rice of today. Points where sources disagree or are thin are marked **⚠️**.

## 1. Before the Kingdoms: Rice in Prehistoric Thailand

Rice is old in Thailand, though not as old as was once claimed. Domesticated rice reached central Thailand about 4,000 years ago, while on the peninsula rice agriculture is attested from roughly 2,500–2,200 years ago ([Wikipedia – Prehistoric Thailand](https://www.Wikipedia.com/wiki/Prehistoric_Thailand)). The excavations at **Non Nok Tha** and **Ban Chiang** in the northeast defined a previously unknown prehistoric cultural tradition ([Penn Museum – Expedition](https://www.penn.museum/sites/expedition/archaeological-survey-and-excavation-of-ban-chiang-culture-sites-in-northeast-thailand/)). The UNESCO dossier for Ban Chiang describes an early farming settlement practising an "elementary form of dry-rice cultivation" ([UNESCO](https://whc.unesco.org/document/153797)). ⚠️ The dates of both sites have been fiercely debated: newer collagen dating at Non Nok Tha places the first Neolithic settlement later than early excavators believed, and some claims of very early rice rest only on rice-tempered pottery ([Higham, via CORE](https://core.ac.uk/download/524779591.pdf)).

On the coast, **Khok Phanom Di**, a deep shell midden on the Bang Pakong estuary dated roughly 2300–1700 BCE, is a key site where rice-farming migrants mingled with indigenous hunter-gatherers ([Wikipedia mirror – Khok Phanom Di](https://wiki.krisyotam.com/content/wikipedia_en_all_nopic_2026-03/Khok_Phanom_Di)). Grain studies from four later sites (Khao Sam Kaeo, Phu Khao Thong, Ban Non Wat and Noen U-Loke) identify the rice as *Oryza sativa japonica* in the late prehistoric period ([Castillo et al., *Rice*, Springer](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s12284-011-9070-2)). ⚠️ Whether the earliest rice was glutinous (sticky) has not been settled by the sources found.

## 2. Dvaravati, the Mon and the Khmer

In the first millennium CE, the Mon-speaking **Dvaravati** culture of the Chao Phraya valley rested on wet-rice farming in the river plains. At Dong Lakhon, a Dvaravati town, excavators recovered carbonised rice grains ([Ancient History Sites – Dong Lakhon](https://www.ancient-history-sites.com/?p=133577)). In a 1965 *Journal of the Siam Society* paper, Gordon Luce argued from linguistic evidence that India "exchanged" Buddhism for rice ([Siam Society](https://thesiamsociety.org/knowledge-hub/research/262?lang=en)). From the 10th century the valley came under **Angkor**, whose kings had inherited an elaborate agricultural system from Funan ([Country Studies – The Mon and the Khmer](https://countrystudies.us/thailand/4.htm)). The Mon left one celebrated rice dish to the Thai court: [khao chae](recipe_khao_chae.md), rice in cool scented water. Thai scholars also trace *khanom chin* rice noodles to the Mon ([Dusit Thani College Journal 18(2), 2024](https://so01.tci-thaijo.org/index.php/journaldtc/article/download/276077/177807/1092579)).

## 3. Sukhothai: "In the Fields There Is Rice"

The most quoted line in Thai history comes from the **Ram Khamhaeng Inscription**, attributed to King Ram Khamhaeng of Sukhothai (late 13th century): *nai nam mi pla, nai na mi khao*, "in the water there are fish, in the fields there is rice". The line still defines Thai abundance. ⚠️ The stele itself is contested. From the late 1980s, Michael Vickery and others argued it was a later fabrication, possibly from the 19th century. The debate is not definitively settled, though most Thai scholars accept it, and electron microscopy has suggested the stone is as old as claimed ([Wikipedia – Ram Khamhaeng Inscription](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ram_Khamhaeng_Inscription)).

Sukhothai is also the traditional origin of the **Royal Ploughing Ceremony**, a Brahmanical rite that opens the rice season. Sources disagree on how far back it can be documented ⚠️: some date it to Sukhothai, others say it became regular only under Ayutthaya. It lapsed after 1936 and was revived in 1960 ([Thailand Foundation](https://thailandfoundation.or.th/royal-ploughing-ceremony/); [My Sakon Nakhon](https://mysakonnakhon.com/the-royal-ploughing-ceremony-in-thailand-a-history/)). Behind it stands **Mae Phosop**, the rice mother (see the ritual rices [khao pradap din](recipe_khao_pradap_din.md) and [khao mathu payat](recipe_khao_mathu_payat.md)): the *khwan* (soul) of rice, a goddess of older animist belief rather than of the Buddhist pantheon ([Wikipedia – Phosop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phosop); [SEAsia – Phosop rice rituals](https://seasia.co/2025/07/17/phosop-rice-rituals-honoring-thailands-sacred-rice-goddess)).

## 4. Lanna and the Sticky-Rice North

Northward, the kingdom of **Lanna**, "a million rice fields", once reached into today's Laos ([Facts and Details – Lanna](https://factsanddetails.com/southeast-asia/Thailand/sub5_8a/entry-6628.html)). Its people, like the Lao and the Thai-Lao of Isan, ate **glutinous rice** (*khao niao*) as their staple. A Chiang Mai food-culture study calls sticky rice the Lanna "main energy dish" ([*Food Culture in Lanna Way of Life*](https://li01.tci-thaijo.org/index.php/sdust/article/view/183834)). Michelin cites academics who suggest glutinous rice may be one of the "original" Thai foods, shared with the Dai of southwest China and the Lowland Lao. It also notes that sticky rice was once used by Central Thais to look down on northerners, who in turn made it a badge of regional pride ([Michelin Guide – Why sticky rice reigns in the north](https://guide.michelin.com/th/en/article/features/why-sticky-rice-reigns-in-the-north-of-thailand)). The resulting map is still the defining divide of Thai rice: **sticky rice in the north and northeast, non-glutinous rice in the centre and south**.

## 5. Ayutthaya: Granary, Port and Court (1351–1767)

**Ayutthaya** was a rice state and a trading city. The Dutch VOC arrived in 1604 and opened a factory in 1608. It sought rice, sappanwood, tin and deer hides, and after the 1664 treaty and the reopening of south Chinese ports, Sino-Siamese trade, "especially the export of rice", intensified ([Thai MFA outreach chronology, ed. Dhiravat na Pombejra](https://image.mfa.go.th/mfa/0/jgapjwQOLy/MFA_outreach_ลพบุรี-อยุธยา/MFA_outreach_chronology_1.pdf); [Ayutthaya History – Dutch settlement](https://ayutthaya-history.com/dutch-settlement.html)).

The city was cosmopolitan. Persians rose high at court: Sheikh Ahmad Qomi, from Qom, became head of Siam's Muslims in the early 17th century and founded the Bunnag line ([Wikipedia – Sheikh Ahmad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheikh_Ahmad_(nobleman_of_Siam))). Persian left *khao buri* ("biryani") in the Thai vocabulary ([Thai PBS World](https://www.thaipbsworld.com/travel-tourism/iran)). From these communities came the court's spiced pilaf, recorded later as [khao hung prung yang thet](recipe_khao_hung_prung_yang_thet.md). Chinese settlers brought pork husbandry, the wok and the spatula, Muslim traders brought coconut milk, and the Portuguese brought egg-based sweets ([Dusit Thani College Journal 18(2), 2024](https://so01.tci-thaijo.org/index.php/journaldtc/article/download/276077/177807/1092579)). ⚠️ The famous Portuguese-Japanese cook Maria Guyomar de Pinha is credited with egg-yolk desserts, not rice dishes.

The best eyewitness of everyday rice is **Simon de La Loubère**, Louis XIV's envoy in 1687–88, whose *Du Royaume de Siam* appeared in 1691 (English 1693). He found that "their common Food is Rice and Fish". A labourer made "a very good Meal with a pound of Rice a day" and "a little dry or salt Fish". Sauces were plain, except for a prized "liquid Sauce, like Mustard, which is only Cray-Fish corrupted", the shrimp paste *kapi* ([Part II, Chap. IV](https://vajirayana.org/v2/asia/a-new-historical-relation-of-the-kingdom-of-siam/part-ii-of-the-manners-of-the-siameses-in-general/chap-iv)). He admired how the Siamese "measure the water, fire and time necessary to the Rice, without bursting the Grain" ([Part I, Chap. VII](https://vajirayana.org/v2/asia/a-new-historical-relation-of-the-kingdom-of-siam/part-i-of-the-country-of-siam/chap-vii-of-the-grain-of-siam)), and his crew regretted going back to bread after three months of rice ([Le Cordon Bleu](https://cordonbleu.edu/news/sawasdee-france/en)). See [rice and dry fish of Ayutthaya](recipe_khao_kap_pla_haeng_ayutthaya.md) and [khao hung chet nam](recipe_khao_hung_chet_nam.md).

## 6. Bangkok's Court Kitchens: From a Royal Poem to the First Cookbooks

After Ayutthaya fell in 1767 and Thonburi's brief interlude, the new Chakri court at Bangkok inherited Ayutthaya's kitchens. The richest early record is a poem: **_Kap He Chom Khrueang Khao Wan_**, a royal barge song composed around 1800 by the future **King Rama II**, praising savoury dishes, fruits and sweets, read as a love poem to Princess Bunrot, herself a renowned cook ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kap_He_Chom_Khrueang_Khao_Wan)). A 2024 Dusit Thani College study turned its 15 savoury dishes into standard recipes. Among them is a "rice seasoned in the foreign manner", which the study identifies as a Persian *pilau/polow* of the early court, distinct from the later Indian-style *khao mok kai* ([Dusit Thani College Journal 18(2), 2024](https://so01.tci-thaijo.org/index.php/journaldtc/article/download/276077/177807/1092579)).

**Khao chae**, chilled rice in jasmine-scented water with elaborate side dishes, is the other great court rice. It came from the Mon, and sources disagree over whether it entered the palace under Rama II or under Rama IV through his Mon consort, Chao Chom Manda Klin ⚠️. Before ice, the water was cooled in earthenware jars in the shade ([Wikipedia – Khao chae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khao_chae)). See [khao chae](recipe_khao_chae.md).

Printed cookbooks followed. **_Mae Khrua Hua Pa_** (1908) by Than Phu Ying Plean Phatsakorawong is widely regarded as Thailand's first cookbook, and it contains the first written massaman recipe ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mae_Khrua_Hua_Pa)). ⚠️ One archive argues that an 1890 pamphlet, *Tamra Gap Khao* by Mom Som Chin, came first ([Thaifoodmaster](https://thaifoodmaster.com/siamese-recipe-archive/81561)). Thai sources also disagree over whether *Mae Khrua Hua Pa* appeared in 1908 or 1909 ([Matichon](https://www.matichon.co.th/prachachuen/news_4834488); [Silpa-mag](https://www.silpa-mag.com/?p=6578)). In the following decades, noble families printed cookbooks as cremation keepsakes, such as *Tamrap Sai Yaowapa* for Princess Yaovabha Bongsanid in 1935 ([Silpa-mag](https://www.silpa-mag.com/?p=42563)). These books preserve court rices that are now forgotten: a Javanese-inspired [khao nasi prajit](recipe_khao_nasi_prajit.md) (c. 1901), fried rice baked in a banana blossom ([khao plee](recipe_khao_bplee.md), 1935), and the palace fried rices of 1935–49 ([khao phat chao wang](recipe_khao_phat_chao_wang.md)).

## 7. The Bowring Treaty and the Rice Frontier

The **Bowring Treaty** of 18 April 1855 opened Siam to free trade, and rice was its great beneficiary. There had been "virtually no export of rice before 1855", but by the century's end rice made up nearly 70 % of Siam's exports ([Wikipedia – Bowring Treaty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bowring_Treaty); [KMUTT – King Mongkut Studies](https://www.lib.kmutt.ac.th/?p=66564)). Exports rose from under a million *hap* a year to about 26 million by 1951 ([KMUTT](https://www.lib.kmutt.ac.th/?p=66564)). The frontier was opened by canals. More than 15 canals linking Bangkok to its hinterland were dug between 1860 and 1910, including the Rangsit scheme of the 1890s, and until World War II some 70–80 % of export rice reached Bangkok by boat ([Kyoto *Southeast Asian Studies*](https://kyoto-seas.org/pdf/36/4/360401.pdf)). The trade was largely Sino-Thai. Families such as the Wang Lee, who arrived in the 1850s, built rice-milling and exporting houses, and the Thai Rice Exporters Association remains a largely Sino-Thai club ([South China Morning Post](https://amp.scmp.com/lifestyle/food-drink/article/2172144/how-chinese-thais-came-dominate-thailands-rice-export-business)). The Chinese-Thai shophouse gave Bangkok its one-plate rices, rice with toppings eaten at a counter. ⚠️ No dated history of specific dishes such as *khao man kai* was found.

## 8. Hom Mali: The Jasmine Rice

Thailand's signature rice is recent. **Khao Dawk Mali 105** traces to a fragrant variety from Bang Khla, Chachoengsao, around 1954. The Rice Department's committee approved and named it in 1959, and distribution to farmers followed, notably from Surin in the northeast in 1961 ([Thai PBS World](https://www.thaipbsworld.com/things-you-may-not-know-about-thai-hom-mali-the-worlds-best-rice/)). ⚠️ Origin dates rest on few sources. Thailand registered **Khao Hom Mali Thung Kula Rong-Hai** as a geographical indication (2006 or 2007, sources differ), and the EU registered it as a PGI in 2013, restricted to KDML 105 and RD15 grown on the Thung Kula Rong-Hai plain ([UK government GI dossier](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6658506ed470e3279dd333b3/Khao_Hom_MAli_Thung_Kula_Rong-Hai.pdf)).

## 9. Rice Today

Thailand was long the world's largest rice exporter. It lost the top place in 2012, after a costly government rice-pledging scheme ([South China Morning Post](https://amp.scmp.com/lifestyle/food-drink/article/2172144/how-chinese-thais-came-dominate-thailands-rice-export-business)), but it still sells hundreds of thousands of tonnes a year to China alone ([Trading Economics](https://tradingeconomics.com/thailand/exports/china/rice)). At home the old map holds:
*   **Sticky rice in the north and Isan**, steamed in a woven basket and eaten by hand ([khao niao nueng](recipe_khao_niao_nueng.md)).
*   **Jasmine rice in the centre**, eaten from a plate with *kap khao* ([khao rad kaeng](recipe_khao_rad_kaeng.md)).
*   **Southern rices**, often dressed with herbs and fermented fish sauces ([khao yam](recipe_khao_yam.md)).
*   **The Chinese-Thai street kitchen** of rice soups, fried rices and rice with toppings ([khao tom](recipe_khao_tom.md), [khao phat](recipe_khao_phat.md), [khao man kai](recipe_khao_man_kai.md), [khao kha mu](recipe_khao_kha_mu.md)).

The rice remains the meal, and everything else accompanies it.
