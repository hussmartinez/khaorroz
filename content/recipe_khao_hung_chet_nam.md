# Khao Hung Chet Nam (Old Siamese Drained Rice) / ข้าวหุงเช็ดน้ำ

> ⬅ [README](README.md) · [Central Thailand](provincial_central_thailand.md)

> ⚠️ **Source-confidence note:** The method, boiling rice in plenty of water, draining it, then finishing it dry over low heat (*dong khao*), is attested by La Loubère's 1680s account of Siamese rice cookery and by living practice recorded in Thai media. It is the standard pre-electric method of central Thailand. No old cookbook quantities were found, so the timings and amounts are a modern reconstruction, tested against the described steps.

## 1. Geographic & Texture Profile
*   **Primary Geographic Zone:** The non-glutinous rice country of the Chao Phraya plain (Ayutthaya, Bangkok, Sukhothai) and, historically, all of central Siam.
*   **Historical Source:** *Du Royaume de Siam*, Simon de La Loubère, 1691 (Ayutthaya, 1687–88): "they measure the water, fire and time necessary to the Rice, without bursting the Grain"; living practice recorded in Sukhothai (Naewna, 2024)
*   **Dish Type & Texture:** Plain boiled-and-drained white rice, the everyday *khao suai*. Separate, unbroken grains, drier and less sticky than absorption-cooked rice, with a toasty aroma from the *dong* (drying) stage.
*   **Traditional Cooking Vessel & Fuel:** An earthenware or later aluminium pot (*mo khao*) on a wood-fired clay stove (*tao*). A wooden paddle stirs the rice, and the lid is held askew to drain.

## 2. Historical & Cultural Context
La Loubère, writing of Ayutthaya, admired how the Siamese "measure the water, fire and time necessary to the Rice, without bursting the Grain" and noted that "generally they boil the Rice in clear water". He confessed that "the Rice boil'd in pure water, how insipid soever, was more agreeable to me" than European bread ([La Loubère, Part I, Chap. VII, via Vajirayana](https://vajirayana.org/v2/asia/a-new-historical-relation-of-the-kingdom-of-siam/part-i-of-the-country-of-siam/chap-vii-of-the-grain-of-siam)). The Thai name for the technique is *hung khao chet nam*: "cooking rice by wiping off the water". After boiling, the starchy water is poured off, and the pot is then rotated over a low flame to steam the rice dry, a stage called ***dong khao***.

The drained water, *nam khao*, was not wasted. It was drunk with a little salt, fed to children and the sick, or used to starch cloth. A 2024 Thai news profile of a 70-year-old cook in Sukhothai, who still uses the method on a wood stove and leaves two electric cookers unused, describes exactly these steps and says older people drank the rice water: "a little salt is enough to make it tasty" ([Naewna, 17 Dec 2024](https://www.naewna.com/likesara/848014)).

**Why drain?** Boiling in excess water and pouring it off removes surface starch, so the grains stay separate. This was a virtue for older, less uniform rices cooked over unpredictable wood fires, and it is how La Loubère's "without bursting the Grain" was achieved.

## 3. Local Town-by-Town Variations (Crucial)
*   **Ayutthaya (17th c., La Loubère):** Rice boiled "in clear water", shaped into a paste-like ball with the fingertips to eat. Occasionally "boil[ed] with milk, as we do on fasting days" ⚠️ (which milk is not stated).
*   **Sukhothai (living practice, 2024):** Rinse, boil while stirring so the rice doesn't stick, drain with the lid held, then *dong* on low heat while rotating the pot.
*   **Central Plains households before the 1960s:** Same method in aluminium pots on charcoal stoves. It gave way to absorption cooking and, from the 1960s–70s, to the electric rice cooker ⚠️ (dating approximate).
*   **North and Isan:** Not used for the staple, which there is *khao niao* (glutinous rice), soaked and steamed in a woven basket (*huad*) over a pot.

## 4. The Master Recipe (4-Person Batch Blueprint)
*Modern adaptation for jasmine or ordinary long-grain Thai rice on a gas or wood flame.*

### The Flavor Base (El Fondo)
| Ingredient | Quantity |
|---|---|
| Thai long-grain white rice (Hom Mali or ordinary *khao khao*) | 400 g |
| Water for boiling | 3 L |
| Salt for the drained rice water (optional) | 2 g |

### Assembly Ratios
| Element | Quantity |
|---|---|
| Rice : boiling water | about 1 : 7 by volume (excess) |
| Boil time | 9–12 min (until the grain is soft but has a tiny white core) |
| *Dong* (drying) time | 10–15 min over very low heat |

### Step-by-Step Cooking Blueprint
1. **Rinse:** Wash the rice 2–3 times in cold water and pour the washing water away.
2. **Boil:** Bring the 3 L of water to a rolling boil, add the rice, and stir gently with a wooden paddle every minute or two so it doesn't settle.
3. **Test:** From about 9 minutes, bite a grain. When it is soft with only a pin-point of white in the centre, take the pot off the heat.
4. **Drain (*chet nam*):** Hold the lid slightly askew and pour off all the starchy water into a bowl. Keep it as *nam khao* and season with a pinch of salt to drink.
5. **Dry (*dong khao*):** Set the covered pot on the lowest flame. Every 2–3 minutes, tilt and rotate the pot (or turn it on embers) so every side dries evenly. After 10–15 minutes the grains are fluffy and separate, with a faint toasty aroma.
6. **Serve:** Fluff with the paddle and serve with curries, relishes and fish (see [rice and dry fish of Ayutthaya](recipe_khao_kap_pla_haeng_ayutthaya.md)).
