# Khao Hung Prung Yang Thet (Rama II's Persian-Style Spiced Rice) / ข้าวหุงปรุงอย่างเทศ

> ⬅ [README](README.md) · [Central Thailand](provincial_central_thailand.md)

> ⚠️ **Source-confidence note:** The dish is named in King Rama II's *Kap He Chom Khrueang Khao Wan* (composed around 1800) as *khao hung prung yang thet*, "rice cooked and seasoned in the foreign manner". The poem gives no recipe. The identification as a Persian pilaf (*pilau/polow*) of the early Bangkok court, inherited from Ayutthaya and distinct from the later Indian-style *khao mok kai*, comes from a 2024 Dusit Thani College study that reconstructed the poem's 15 savoury dishes. The full text of that study's standard recipe was not accessible here, so the ingredient list below is a reconstruction based on its description (spices, vegetables and several grains or pulses) and on Persian *polow* technique.

## 1. Geographic & Texture Profile
*   **Primary Geographic Zone:** The royal kitchens of early Rattanakosin Bangkok (and, by inheritance, Ayutthaya's court and its Persian community).
*   **Historical Source:** *Kap He Chom Khrueang Khao Wan* (กาพย์เห่ชมเครื่องคาวหวาน), King Phutthaloetla Naphalai (Rama II, then Prince Itsarasunthon), c. 1800, Bangkok; interpreted in Dusit Thani College Journal 18(2), 2024
*   **Dish Type & Texture:** Court spiced rice in the Persian pilaf manner. Separate, fragrant grains cooked in ghee and stock with whole spices, with vegetables and grains or pulses folded through.
*   **Traditional Cooking Vessel & Fuel:** A heavy brass or copper pot with a tight lid, set on charcoal with embers on the lid (the *dum*/steam-sealed finish used for pilaf).

## 2. Historical & Cultural Context
*Kap He Chom Khrueang Khao Wan* is a royal barge-procession song in which the future Rama II praises a procession of savoury dishes, fruits and sweets, traditionally read as a love poem to Princess Bunrot (later Queen Si Suriyendra), herself famed as a cook ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kap_He_Chom_Khrueang_Khao_Wan)). Among the savoury dishes is a rice "*prung yang thet*", seasoned in the foreign (*thet*, i.e. Persian or Indian Muslim) way.

The 2024 Dusit Thani College study, which turned all 15 savoury dishes of the poem into standard recipes, explains that this *khao hung* "is rice cooked with spices, vegetables and many kinds of grain, originally a dish of the *khaek thet* or Persians, like the Muslim *khao mok*". The Persian *khao mok* that was a court dish of early Rattanakosin, inherited from Ayutthaya, was the recipe called ***khao pilau*** or ***polow***, while "the *khao mok kai* that Thais know today came from India in a later period" ([Dusit Thani College Journal 18(2), 2024](https://so01.tci-thaijo.org/index.php/journaldtc/article/download/276077/177807/1092579)). The same review notes that coconut milk entered Thai cooking with Muslim traders, and that massaman, another of the poem's dishes, is a Malay-influenced fusion.

The Persian presence behind the dish is well documented. Sheikh Ahmad Qomi, a merchant from Qom, rose at the Ayutthaya court in the early 17th century to become *Chularatchamontri*, head of the kingdom's Muslims. His descendants founded the Bunnag noble family ([Wikipedia – Sheikh Ahmad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheikh_Ahmad_(nobleman_of_Siam)); [Wikipedia – Bunnag family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bunnag_family)). Persian left words in Thai food vocabulary, including *khao buri* for biryani ([Thai PBS World, 2025](https://www.thaipbsworld.com/travel-tourism/iran)).

## 3. Local Town-by-Town Variations (Crucial)
*   **Early Bangkok court (c. 1800):** The poem's *khao hung prung yang thet*, a Persian *polow*-type rice per the 2024 study.
*   **Ayutthaya Persian community (17th c.):** The presumed source of the court recipe ⚠️ (the inheritance is asserted by the 2024 study; no Ayutthaya-era recipe text was found).
*   **Later Indian-style [*khao mok kai*](recipe_khao_mok_kai.md):** Chicken biryani with turmeric and dried spices, served with sweet-sour green sauce. It arrived later and is now a Thai-Muslim street and southern staple (a separate modern dish).
*   ***Khao buri*:** The Persian-derived Thai name for biryani that survives in vocabulary ⚠️ (usage today is rare).

## 4. The Master Recipe (4-Person Batch Blueprint)
*Modern reconstruction of a court* polow *following the 2024 study's description (spices, vegetables, grains): long-grain rice, ghee, whole spices, chickpeas, peas and carrot, finished under a sealed lid.*

### The Flavor Base (El Fondo)
| Ingredient | Quantity |
|---|---|
| Ghee (or clarified butter) | 60 g |
| Shallots, thinly sliced | 120 g |
| Cardamom pods, lightly crushed | 6 |
| Cinnamon stick | 1 (5 cm) |
| Cloves | 4 |
| Bay leaves | 2 |
| Cumin seed | 3 g |
| Chicken or light meat stock, hot | 800 ml |
| Salt | 8 g |

### Assembly Ratios
| Element | Quantity |
|---|---|
| Long-grain rice (jasmine or basmati-type), soaked 30 min | 400 g |
| Cooked chickpeas | 150 g |
| Green peas | 100 g |
| Carrot, small dice | 100 g |
| Raisins (optional, Persian touch) | 40 g |
| Toasted almonds or cashews | 40 g |
| Stock : rice | 2 : 1 by weight |

### Step-by-Step Cooking Blueprint
1. **Fry the aromatics:** Melt the ghee in a heavy pot. Fry the shallots slowly until deep golden, then lift out half for garnish.
2. **Bloom the spices:** Add the cardamom, cinnamon, cloves, bay and cumin to the pot and fry for 1 minute until fragrant.
3. **Toast the rice:** Drain the soaked rice, add it with the carrot, and stir for 2–3 minutes until every grain is coated in ghee.
4. **Cook:** Pour in the hot stock with the salt, chickpeas and raisins, bring to the boil, then cover and cook on low heat for 12 minutes.
5. **Seal and steam:** Scatter the peas on top. Lay a cloth under the lid, seal tightly, and leave on the lowest heat (or with embers on the lid) for 10 minutes. Rest off the heat for 5 minutes.
6. **Serve:** Fluff gently, pick out the whole spices if you like, and top with the reserved shallots and nuts. In the poem's procession it sat beside curries such as massaman.
