# Harisat al-Aruzz (Abbasid Rice Harisa) / هريسة الأرز

> ⬅ [README](README.md) · [Syria](provincial_syria.md)

> ⚠️ **Source-confidence note:** The name *harisat al-aruzz* is attested for a rice harisa in a 13th-century Baghdadi cookery book. Harisa itself is documented in Ibn Sayyar al-Warraq's 10th-century cookbook and in al-Baghdadi (1226). The method below follows the medieval steps as summarised by the food-history site *Eat Like a Sultan* (meat cooked to shreds and pounded, milk, rice, sesame oil and fat beaten to a "nougat-like" consistency, served with *murri*). No quantities survive, so they are reconstructed. Harisa (with wheat) is still a Levantine feast dish; the rice version is lost.

## 1. Geographic & Texture Profile
*   **Primary Geographic Zone:** Abbasid Baghdad (source). Harisa culture runs "from the Levant to the Persian Gulf".
*   **Historical Source:** a 13th-century Baghdadi cookery book (al-Baghdadi, *Kitab al-Tabikh*, 1226), building on Ibn Sayyar al-Warraq, *Kitab al-Tabikh*, 10th century
*   **Dish Type & Texture:** Rice porridge (*aruzziyya*). Meat and rice beaten together into a thick, smooth, elastic porridge, glossy with sesame oil and fat.
*   **Traditional Cooking Vessel & Fuel:** A large cauldron over a slow wood fire, beaten with a heavy wooden paddle.

## 2. Historical & Cultural Context
Rice's earliest role in medieval Arab cooking was in porridges, the *aruzziyyat*. The prestige porridge was *harisa*: meat cooked until it falls apart, pounded, and beaten with grain until it becomes one smooth mass. It was a dish of weddings, Ramadan and Eid, made by the wealthy and customarily shared with poorer neighbours. The wheat version survives across the Levant and the Gulf (*harees*). Rice harisa, made with the costlier grain, is effectively a lost dish.

Following the Eat Like a Sultan summary: fatty meat is cooked in salted water until it falls apart and is pounded in a mortar. Milk is cooked in the broth, rice is added, and then sesame oil and rendered fat. The mass is beaten continually until it reaches a nougat-like consistency, and it is served with *murri*, the medieval fermented-barley condiment, for which soy sauce is a modern stand-in.

**Culinary logic:** long beating releases the rice starch and the meat's gelatin into an emulsion. That's why harisa is elastic rather than mushy, and why it needs strength and time.

## 3. Local Town-by-Town Variations (Crucial)
*   **Baghdad, 10th–13th c.:** Wheat *harisa* and rice *harisa* both recorded.
*   **Levant today:** Wheat *harisa* for Christian feasts and Eid in Lebanon, Syria and Palestine. The rice form is not in living practice ⚠️.
*   **Gulf:** *Harees* (wheat), a Ramadan staple.

## 4. The Master Recipe (4-Person Batch Blueprint)
*Modern adaptation; quantities reconstructed.*

### The Flavor Base (El Fondo)
| Ingredient | Quantity |
|---|---|
| Fatty lamb or goat (shoulder, neck) | 800 g |
| Water | 1.5 L |
| Salt | 1½ tsp |
| Whole milk | 500 ml |
| Sesame oil (untoasted) | 3 tbsp |
| Ghee or rendered fat | 3 tbsp |
| Soy sauce (for murri), to serve | 2 tbsp |

### Assembly Ratios
| Element | Quantity |
|---|---|
| Short-grain rice, washed | 200 g |
| Liquid at rice-in (broth + milk) | ≈ 1.2 L |

### Step-by-Step Cooking Blueprint
1. **Meat:** Simmer the meat in the salted water until it falls from the bone (**2–2.5 h**). Lift it out, remove the bones and pound or shred it finely.
2. **Milk and rice:** Strain the broth and keep about 700 ml. Add the milk, bring to a simmer, add the rice and cook stirring often until very soft (**30 min**).
3. **Beat:** Return the meat and add the sesame oil and ghee. Beat vigorously with a wooden spoon over low heat for **15–20 min**, until smooth, glossy and elastic.
4. **Serve:** Spread in a dish, make a well, add a little extra melted ghee and serve with the soy "murri" on the side.
