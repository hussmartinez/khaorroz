# A History of Rice in Bilad al-Sham / تاريخ الأرز في بلاد الشام

> ⬅ [README](README.md)

A sourced narrative of how rice reached the Levant (Palestine, Lebanon, Jordan and Syria), why it was long a grain of cities and feasts while bulgur and freekeh fed the countryside, how it travelled by ship from Egypt's Nile Delta, and how dishes such as *maqluba*, *mansaf* and *mujaddara* became symbols of hospitality, faith and identity. It also traces the thread that links this kitchen to Spain: the Arabic word *ar-ruzz* became Spanish *arroz*. Points where sources disagree or are thin are marked **⚠️**.

## 1. A Late-Arriving Grain

Rice was domesticated in Asia and reached West Asia slowly. A 2021 archaeobotanical review concludes that rice was present in West Asia and Europe by the end of the first millennium BC, but **did not become a significant crop in West Asia until the past few centuries** ([Stevens & Fuller et al., *A Journey to the West*, Rice 2021](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8464642/)). Domesticated rice moved from South Asia across the Iranian plateau towards western Iran and Mesopotamia ([UCL short report](https://student-journals.ucl.ac.uk/pia/article/213/galley/313/view)). Finds on Iran's southern Caspian coast date to around 120 BC, and the Roman army was eating imported rice at Novaesium (Neuss, on the Rhine) in the early 1st century AD. In the Levant itself, rice remained for a long time an imported or locally marginal grain, more often traded than grown ([Arab America – The Rice Revolution](https://arabamerica.com/the-rice-revolution-the-culinary-shift-that-changed-the-health-landscape-of-the-levant)).

The name travelled with the grain. Greek *óryza*, of Indo-Iranian (some argue Dravidian) origin, is the ancestor of Arabic ***aruzz*** (أرز), colloquially ***ruzz*** or ***rizz*** in Levantine speech ([Wiktionary – arroz](https://en.wiktionary.com/wiki/arroz)). ⚠️ The deepest root of the word is disputed.

## 2. The Abbasid Kitchen: Rice in the First Arabic Cookbooks

The first Arabic cookery books come from Baghdad, but they shaped the whole Arab world, Syria included. The earliest is the 10th-century ***Kitab al-Tabikh*** of **Ibn Sayyar al-Warraq**, with more than 600 recipes in 132 chapters ([Wikipedia – Ibn Sayyar al-Warraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_Sayyar_al-Warraq); [Aramco World review of Nawal Nasrallah's translation *Annals of the Caliphs' Kitchens*](https://www.aramcoworld.com/en/resources/reviews/2017/annals-of-the-caliphs-kitchen-ibn-sayyar-alwarraqs-tenthcentury-baghdadi-cookbook)). Its rice is not the fluffy pilaf of today. According to the food historian Charles Perry, rice and wheat were "often cooked in the *tannuur* overnight with meat and spices to make a sort of meaty porridge" (*aruzziyyah*, *hintiyyah*). The boil-then-steam pilaf technique was unknown, and the book contains no pilaf recipe. Rice also appears in sweets: *muhallabiyyah* was "a pudding sometimes thickened with rice flour, like the present-day version" ([Perry, "Cooking with the Caliphs", Saudi Aramco World, 2006](https://archive.aramcoworld.com/issue/200604/cooking.with.the.caliphs.htm)). Perry also notes that the Baghdadi corpus has no bulgur, which is a reminder that the familiar Levantine grain hierarchy came later.

The second Baghdad book, **Muhammad ibn al-Hasan al-Baghdadi**'s *Kitab al-Tabikh*, was completed in 1226 ([Wikipedia – al-Baghdadi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_bin_Hasan_al-Baghdadi); [Perry's translation, *A Baghdad Cookery Book*](https://equinoxpub.com/projects/baghdad-cookery-book)). It contains a dish called ***maqluba*** ("turned upside down"), and modern encyclopaedias cite it as the earliest written ancestor of the [maqluba](recipe_maqluba.md) now eaten across the Levant ([Wikipedia – Maqluba](https://en.wikipedia.com/wiki/Maqluba)). ⚠️ The medieval maqluba was a layered meat-and-vegetable dish. Whether its build matches today's rice-heavy version is not established, and the popular story that Saladin named the dish after the liberation of Jerusalem has **no documentary support** in the sources found.

**Syria's own medieval cookbook.** In the same century Aleppo produced ***Kitab al-Wusla ila al-Habib*** ("Winning the Beloved's Heart with Delectable Dishes and Perfumes"), sometimes attributed to the Aleppine historian **Ibn al-'Adim**. The University of Aleppo published an Arabic edition in 1986, and an English translation appeared in 2017 as *Scents and Flavors: A Syrian Cookbook* ([Wikipedia – Kitab al-Wuslah ila l-habib](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kitab_al-Wuslah_ila_l-habib)). Together with al-Baghdadi's book it is credited with some of the earliest known pilaf-type rice recipes. ⚠️ The attribution to Ibn al-'Adim is uncertain.

## 3. A Grain of Cities and Feasts: Rice, Bulgur and Freekeh

For most of the last thousand years, the everyday grains of Levantine villages were **wheat products**: bread, ***burghul*** (bulgur, parboiled and cracked wheat) and ***freekeh*** (green wheat roasted in the field). Rice was a purchased commodity. It was associated with towns, merchants and festive tables. Arabic food writing describes bulgur as the Levant's main substitute for rice ([Mawdoo3 – mujaddara with bulgur](https://mawdoo3.com/طريقة_طبخ_المجدرة_بالبرغل)). The hardships of the Syrian war have made bulgur a staple again in rural Daraa ([Enab Baladi, 2022](https://english.enabbaladi.net/archives/2022/08/living-hardships-make-bulgur-staple-a-must-in-southern-syria/)).

Two of today's emblematic rice dishes show the shift:

*   **[Mujaddara](recipe_mujaddara.md)**: Arabic recipe sites say the dish was formerly made with bulgur, lentils and onion, and that rice replaced bulgur once it became common in the Shami kitchen ([Mawdoo3](https://mawdoo3.com/طريقة_عمل_المجدرة_على_أصولها)). Both versions survive. In Lebanon, *mjaddara* is usually the puréed form, while the whole-grain version is called *mudardara* ([Wikipedia – Mujaddara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mujaddara)).
*   **[Mansaf](recipe_mansaf.md)**: originally a pastoralist Bedouin dish of meat and *jameed* (dried yoghurt) over bread and bulgur, it changed substantially in the 20th century. Rice entered northern Transjordanian mansaf in the **1920s**, first mixed with bulgur and later on its own ([Wikipedia – Mansaf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mansaf)). ⚠️ A mirror of the same article places the change around the 1950s, so the date is uncertain. The historian Joseph Massad has argued that the dish's "traditional" form is itself partly modern.

A 2021 overview of rice dispersal likewise stresses that rice became a major West Asian crop only in recent centuries ([Stevens & Fuller et al.](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8464642/)). Studies of rural and urban foodways in Syria after the First World War show how class and region shaped what was eaten ([Pekow, *From Farm to Table*](https://libmast.utm.my/Record/doaj.art-a9df2bab86284e86a49185a1d63ad340)). ⚠️ No source found states the rice-for-towns and bulgur-for-villages division as a hard rule. It is a tendency, not a law, and urban Aleppo and Damascus cooked plenty of bulgur.

## 4. Ottoman Rice: Ships from Damietta, Paddies in the Marshes

Under the Ottomans (1516–1918) much of the Levant's rice came by sea from **Egypt**. Rice was the main cash crop of the northern Nile Delta until cotton rose in the mid-19th century. Its caloric density and long shelf life made it a strategic and luxury good for the Ottoman elite ([MESA abstract on 18th-century Egyptian rice](https://my-mesa.org/program/abstracts/view/eyJpdiI6IjA1azFhaG1YSVNZbzRqSWFpdlFpRGc9PSIsInZhbHVlIjoiOWIycjVBWVpBSmRnYnNZUzk1RzNjUT09IiwibWFjIjoiZmE3NDU2NzVhN2QwZTI3NmZiMmEyMWRlYWJiN2FlYTQzZDgxYWNjNjc5ZTFkYmVjYmRlMjhlY2ZkOTJhMGI2OSIsInRhZyI6IiJ9)). The port of **Damietta** shipped rice, coffee, sugar and linen "to Anatolian coasts, **Syrian ports** and the Ottoman capital" ([DergiPark, 2022](https://dergipark.org.tr/en/pub/nevsosbilen/issue/72869/1137819)). An 1810 *Encyclopaedia Britannica* noted that Christians from **Aleppo and Damascus** settled in Damietta had "for several ages carried on its principal commerce" ([EB 1810](https://jimclifford.ca/early_encyclopedia_britannica/articles/1810/eb_4th_1810_014118.html)). The Egyptian connection survives in the kitchen: Palestinian recipes for maqluba still call for ***ruzz masri***, Egyptian short-grain rice, often mixed with long-grain ([Mawdoo3 – Palestinian maqluba](https://mawdoo3.com/المقلوبة_الفلسطينية)).

Rice was also grown locally where water allowed. In the **Hula Valley** of northern Palestine, rice is reported as a traditional crop going back to the Hellenistic period. In the 19th century the **Ghawarna** of the Hula swamps herded water buffalo, grew rice and wheat and wove reed mats, until the marsh was drained in the 1950s ([Jewish Virtual Library – Hula Valley](https://www.jewishvirtuallibrary.org/hula-valley); [Hula Lake](https://wiki.riteme.site/wiki/Hula_Lake)). ⚠️ The sources are encyclopaedic. Comparable marsh rice in Syria's Ghab or the Amuq plain is plausible, but no source found documents it, so it is not claimed here. Elsewhere in the empire, Ottoman rice plantations reshaped the Balkans ([Shopov, "Cities of Rice"](https://orb.binghamton.edu/history_fac/1)).

## 5. Feasts, Hospitality and the Religious Calendar

By the 20th century, rice had become the centre of the Levantine feast. The great platters are built on it:

*   **[Mansaf](recipe_mansaf.md)**: lamb in *jameed* sauce over rice and thin *shrak* bread, eaten standing around a shared tray. UNESCO inscribed ***Al-Mansaf in Jordan, a festive banquet and its social and cultural meanings*** on its Representative List of Intangible Cultural Heritage at its 17th session in Rabat in November–December 2022 ([UNESCO decision 17.COM 7.B.19](https://ich.unesco.org/en/decisions/17.COM/7.B.19); [Arab News](https://arabnews.com/node/2208976/food-health)). The committee cited its role in hospitality, solidarity and social cohesion.
*   **[Maqluba](recipe_maqluba.md)**: the upside-down pot of rice, meat and fried vegetables, a fixture of Ramadan *iftar* in Palestine and Jordan ([Gulf News – a Nablus-inspired iftar](https://gulfnews.com/going-out/society/a-palestinian-iftar-dinner-in-dubai-inspired-from-nablus-1.2041772)).
*   **[Qidreh](recipe_qidreh.md)**: Hebron's lamb and rice baked in a sealed pot in the baker's oven, for weddings, births, Fridays, Ramadan, both Eids, and Christmas among Palestinian Christians.
*   **[Sayadieh](recipe_sayadieh.md)**: the coast's fishermen's rice, dark with caramelised onion.
*   **[Ouzi](recipe_ouzi.md)**: spiced rice and meat baked inside pastry, a Damascene celebration dish.

The **religious calendar** shapes rice cooking for Muslims and Christians alike. During **Ramadan**, rice-and-meat platters such as mansaf and maqluba anchor the *iftar* table after dates and lentil soup ([The National – the iftar table](https://www.thenationalnews.com/lifestyle/food/the-iftar-table-harees-and-lamb-mansaf-1.42839)). **Eid** leans towards sweets (*ma'amoul*). Among Arab Christians, **Lent** brings the meatless rices: [mujaddara](recipe_mujaddara.md) is traditionally eaten on fast days, and in Lebanon on the first day of Lent ([food-heritage.org – Mujaddara on Ash Monday](https://food-heritage.org/mujaddara-on-ash-monday/)). [Warak enab](recipe_warak_enab.md) *bi-zeit* (vine leaves stuffed with rice, herbs and olive oil) is the oil-based fasting form of the stuffed leaves. Sweet rice closes the circle: [riz bi-haleeb](recipe_riz_bi_haleeb.md), perfumed with mastic and orange blossom, descends from the medieval *muhallabiyyah* family of puddings (see §2).

## 6. The Andalusi Thread: From *ar-Ruzz* to *Arroz*

The Arabic world carried rice west. In al-Andalus, agronomists wrote detailed instructions for growing it. The 12th-century Sevillian **Ibn al-'Awwam** devoted a chapter of his *Kitab al-Filaha* to rice, millet and sesame. He described flooding the field to a set depth before sowing, and recommended eating rice cooked with butter, oil, fat and milk ([Qatar Digital Library – Kitab al-Filaha](https://www.qdl.qa/en/archive/81055/vdc_100000000042.0x0003d4); [Muslim Heritage – Farming Manuals](https://muslimheritage.com/?p=3297)). Spanish ***arroz*** comes from Andalusi Arabic *ar-rawz*, from Arabic *aruzz*, with the Arabic article *al-* fused into the word, just as in *aceite* (oil) or *azafrán* (saffron) ([Wiktionary – arroz](https://en.wiktionary.com/wiki/arroz)). The full Spanish side of the story is told in the [history of rice in Spain](history.md).

The kitchens still rhyme. Ibn al-'Awwam's rice "with milk" sits naturally beside both riz bi-haleeb and the Spanish [arroz con leche](recipe_arroz_con_leche.md). The fishermen's broth rice of [sayadieh](recipe_sayadieh.md) mirrors the Alicante [arroz a banda](recipe_arroz_a_banda.md). Hebron's oven-baked qidreh mirrors the Valencian [arroz al horno](recipe_arroz_al_horno.md), both cooked in the village baker's oven. ⚠️ These are **parallels, not proven lines of descent**. Milk-rice puddings and fish-broth rices arose in many cuisines, and no source found traces a specific Levantine dish directly to a specific Spanish one.

## 7. The Modern Era: Imports, Displacement and Memory

Today almost all of the Levant's rice is imported, and the grain varies by dish and household. In **Jordan**, US rice has been on the market for more than 40 years. Medium-grain **Calrose** from California is the dominant American type, and a 2021 California drought pushed traders towards Arkansas and Louisiana rice ([USA Rice / USAEDC seminar](https://usaedc.org/casestudies/seminar-introduces-new-us-rice-varieties-in-jordan)). Supermarket shelves in Amman carry Calrose next to many **basmati** brands ([Talabat Jordan, rice aisle](https://www.talabat.com/jordan/grocery/640093/jabal-el-shamali-rusaifeh/cooking-baking/rice?aid=5010)). Palestinian home recipes often combine Egyptian short-grain, American and long-grain rice in a single maqluba. ⚠️ No source found quantifies a regional preference for Egyptian over Californian rice, so this is reported as recipe practice only.

The 20th and 21st centuries also brought **displacement**: more than 750,000 Palestinians displaced in 1948 and around 400,000 in 1967, and millions of Syrians since 2011. Cooks and writers describe food, and rice dishes above all, as a way of keeping identity and place. The Palestinian author **Reem Kassis** speaks of food as a source of "rootedness" ([The New Arab](https://www.newarab.com/features/what-role-does-palestinian-cuisine-play-protecting-identity?amp=1)). Cookbooks such as **Sami Tamimi** and Tara Wigley's *Falastin* and **Fadi Kattan**'s *Bethlehem* record maqluba, qidreh and their relatives for a global audience ([Arab News](https://www.arabnews.com/node/2647110/lifestyle); [Gulf News](https://gulfnews.com/food/palestinian-cuisine-its-time-for-maqluba-and-freekeh-to-reach-tables-worldwide-1.1621944763667)). In the diaspora, from Chile to Michigan, recipes are adapted to whatever rice the local shop sells. UNESCO's recognition of mansaf in 2022 marked the moment a Levantine rice feast became officially part of the world's heritage.

## 8. Lost Rices of the Syrian Kitchen: From the *Wusla* to Russell's Aleppo

The decisive step after Baghdad came in 13th-century Syria. The *Kitab al-Wusla ila al-Habib*, compiled under the Ayyubids and in some copies credited to the Aleppine historian Ibn al-ʿAdim ⚠️ (the attribution is uncertain), runs to 635 recipes ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kitab_al-Wuslah_ila_l-habib)). Its rice chapter lists dishes that have since vanished: a *muhallabiyya*, white *bardawili* rice, "marble" *rukhamiyya*, plain and yellow pilafs, rice with cornelian cherries, *faʾiziyya*, a servants' *kashkiyya*, and the "wonderful" [aruzz khatuni](recipe_aruzz_khatuni.md), the Lady's rice glossed with pistachios, sugar and rose water ([UAM catalogue, table of contents](https://biblioteca.izt.uam.mx/vufind/Record/JSTOR_ocn981813198/TOC); [BU gastronomy blog](https://sites.bu.edu/gastronomyblog/2017/11/07/cookbooks-history-syrian-khatuni-rice)). Together with al-Baghdadi's 1226 book it contains the earliest pilaf-type recipes, such as the [aruzz mufalfal of the Wusla](recipe_aruzz_mufalfal_wusla.md), three centuries before the word "pilaf" appears in a Persian cookbook of 1521 ([Wikipedia – Pilaf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pilaf)). Al-Baghdadi also gives the first recorded [mujaddara](recipe_mujaddara_medieval.md), still made with meat ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mujaddara)), and a [shurba](recipe_shurba_al_baghdadi.md) of rice in lamb broth with chickpeas and dill ([Arberry tr., via Cariadoc](https://www.pbm.com/~lindahl/cariadoc/islamic_wo_veggies.html)). Earlier still, Ibn Sayyar al-Warraq's 10th-century *Kitab al-Tabikh* used rice in porridges, puddings and as a thickener. Its rice-and-chicken [muhallabiyya](recipe_muhallabiyya_medieval.md), cooked in milk and glazed with honey, is the ancestor in name of today's [muhallabiyeh](recipe_muhallabiyeh.md), and the prestige porridge [harisat al-aruzz](recipe_harisat_al_aruzz.md) survives only on the page ([Eat Like a Sultan](https://eatlikeasultan.com/abbasid-rice-harisa/); [Gulf News](https://gulfnews.com/food/phirni-muhalabia-and-blancmange--many-names-for-the-same-dessert-1.1610623574357)).

Under the Ottomans the Levantine table took the shape it still has. Alexander Russell, physician to the English factory in Aleppo, wrote in 1756 that a meal opened with soup and closed with "a large pilaw", sometimes followed by a "sweet starch" pudding. In between came birds stuffed with rice, a whole lamb "stuffed with rice, almonds, raisins, pistaches" ([kharuf mahshi](recipe_kharuf_mahshi_halabi.md)), and *mahshee*: vine leaves and vegetables filled with rice and meat. He also noted that the city's Christians ate "more *burgle*, and less rice" ([Russell, *The Natural History of Aleppo*, Project Gutenberg](https://gutenberg.org/cache/epub/77691/pg77691-images.html)). The first printed Ottoman cookbook, Mehmed Kâmil's *Melceü't-Tabbâhîn* (1844), devotes a 13-recipe chapter to pilaf. It was translated into English in 1864 and into Arabic in 1887, and the Arabic version stayed in print until 1915, carrying Istanbul's pilav rules into Arabic-reading kitchens ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melce%C3%BC%27t-Tabb%C3%A2h%C3%AEn)); see the [Ottoman butter pilaf](recipe_pilav_ottoman_aleppo.md). In Palestinian villages, Hilma Granqvist's ethnography of Artas records rice as "rice from the sea", imported through Jaffa. Two sacks of rice could form part of a bride price, and at weddings men cooked meat and rice over big fires and served them on wooden trays ([*Jerusalem Quarterly* 99, 2024](https://ps.boell.org/sites/default/files/2025-06/jerusalem-quarterly-issue-99.pdf)); see [ruz w lahm al-ʿurs](recipe_ruz_w_lahm_al_urs.md).

## 9. Communities Within: Circassians, Chechens and the Jews of Aleppo and Damascus

The Levant's rice traditions were never only Arab. Circassian refugees settled Amman (1878), Wadi al-Seer, Jerash, Na'ur and al-Rusaifa after the 1878 Treaty of Berlin, and the first Chechen families founded Zarqa in 1903 ([Jordan Times](https://jordantimes.com/news/local/refugees-founders-studying-legacy-circassians-chechens-jordans-history); [Cambridge IJMES](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/international-journal-of-middle-east-studies/article/circassian-refugees-and-the-making-of-amman-18781914/B690DDB10A2E125B92C9E75C1485C1CB); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chechens_in_Jordan)). In Jordan, Circassian cooks adapted Caucasian millet and maize dishes to rice and bulgur, as in [ships w pasta](recipe_ships_w_pasta.md) ([Atlas Obscura](https://atlasobscura.com/articles/the-women-keeping-circassian-cuisine-alive-in-jordan)). Aleppo's Jewish community, shaped by Ottoman and Iberian (post-1492) influences, gave the region its sweet-sour stuffed vine leaves, [yebra](recipe_yebra.md), and the ground-rice dumplings of [kibbeh hamda](recipe_kibbeh_hamda.md) ([Hebrew wiki: Aleppine Jewish cuisine](https://www.hamichlol.org.il/%D7%9E%D7%98%D7%91%D7%97_%D7%99%D7%94%D7%95%D7%93%D7%99_%D7%97%D7%9C%D7%91); [Tablet – yebra](https://100jewishfoods.tabletmag.com/yebra)). Those dishes travelled with emigrants to Brooklyn, where an Aleppine family's Passover Seder opened with kibbeh hamda in the 1920s ([Jewish Food Society](https://www.jewishfoodsociety.org/stories/the-syrian-passover-soup-that-came-to-brooklyn)), and to Mexico City, where the community split into Aleppine (Maguén David) and Damascene (Monte Sinaí) congregations ([Wikipedia – Syrian Mexicans](https://wiki.riteme.site/wiki/Syrian_Mexicans)).

## 10. The Mahjar: Chile, Mexico, Brazil and the *Arroz Árabe*

From the 1870s to the 1930s, Christians from Bethlehem, Beit Jala and Beit Sahour, Lebanese villagers and Syrians emigrated to the Americas on Ottoman papers, which is why Latin Americans still call them *turcos* ([Colmex – Historia Mexicana](https://historiamexicana.colmex.mx/index.php/RHM/article/download/2268/2936); [INAH – Dimensión Antropológica](https://revistas.inah.gob.mx/index.php/dimension/article/download/1618/1560/2963); [Revista UFG](https://revistas.ufg.br/historia/article/view/17484)). Brazil's community grew mostly in São Paulo, and the Lebanese of Mexico prospered in Yucatán's henequén boom ([Travessia](https://travessia.emnuvens.com.br/travessia/article/download/1048/942/1947); [Mexico News Daily](https://mexiconewsdaily.com/mexicolife/migrants-legacy-everywhere-yet-easily-missed)). **Chile** holds the largest Palestinian community outside the Arab world, with reports citing 300,000 descendants or more. Its first quarter was Patronato, and the Club Palestino was founded in 1939 ([Wikipedia](https://wikiwand.com/en/articles/Palestinians_in_Chile); [BBC Mundo via MDZ](https://www.mdzol.com/mundo/especial-bbc-mundo-la-minipalestina-chile-una-las-mayores-comunidades-palestinos-fuera-del-mundo-arabe-n1499989)). Recipes travelled orally. A Beit Jala-origin cook in Quillota makes "rice with noodles instead of plain", and wakes end with [fate](recipe_fate_chilena.md), rice with yogurt, cauliflower and goat (Bascuñan-Wiley, *Mashriq & Mahjar* 2019, [PDF](https://lebanesestudies.ojs.chass.ncsu.edu/index.php/mashriq/article/download/239/691)). The Chilean-Palestinian canon is set out in cookbooks from Berr & Yunis's *Lo mejor de la cocina árabe* (1983) to Mujeres Palestinas por Gaza (2024). It rests on [arroz árabe](recipe_arroz_arabe_chileno.md), *hojas de parra* and *rellenos*, with beef for lamb and Chilean vine leaves as substitutions, and in Santiago "comida árabe" simply means "comida palestina" ([*Jerusalem Quarterly* 99](https://ps.boell.org/sites/default/files/2025-06/jerusalem-quarterly-issue-99.pdf)). Across Peru, Chile, Colombia and the Dominican Republic, rice fried with fine noodles, the Levant's [riz bi sh'ariyyeh](recipe_riz_bi_shariyyeh.md), became *arroz árabe*, a Christmas Eve side dish ([Wikipedia – Arabic rice](https://www.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_rice); [La República](https://larepublica.pe/recetas/arroz-arabe-como-preparar-este-acompanamiento-del-pavo-navideno-cena-navidad-atmp-1085966)).
